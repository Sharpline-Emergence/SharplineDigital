Check Your Own Website
in 10 Minutes

By P. Beal — 28 years in banking & financial planning, 19 years as a small business owner, Fraser Valley, BC

Quick Read — Key Takeaways
  • You don’t need to be technical to check your website. You need a phone, a laptop and 10 minutes.
  • Start on your phone. Nearly half of Canadian web traffic is mobile, and local customers in a hurry are almost always on one.
  • The most expensive problem is the quiet one: a contact form that doesn’t deliver. Send yourself a test message.
  • Check that Google can find you, that your hours and phone number are right, and that nothing is broken.
  • Make sure you know who controls your domain and hosting. Many owners don’t, until the day it matters.

Most small business owners haven’t looked at their own website properly since the day it launched. That’s understandable. You’re busy running the business, and the site seems to be “up.” But a website can look fine on your office computer while quietly losing customers on a phone, missing messages, or showing last year’s hours. This checklist takes about 10 minutes, needs no technical knowledge, and tells you whether your site is doing its job.

Grab your phone and a laptop. Work through the eight checks in order, and note anything that fails. There’s a summary table at the end to help you decide what to fix first.

01 / The Phone Test (2 minutes)

Nearly half of all web visits in Canada now happen on a phone.1 For a local service business, the share is often higher: someone with a leaking tap isn’t walking to a desktop. So start where your customers are.

Open your website on your phone, the way a stranger would: search your business name on Google and tap through. Then ask yourself:

  • Can you tell what the business does and where, without scrolling? “Plumbing repairs in Chilliwack” should be obvious on the first screen, not buried three swipes down.
  • Can you read it without pinching and zooming? Tiny text or a page that slides sideways means the site wasn’t built for phones.
  • Tap your phone number. Does your phone offer to dial it? If the number is part of an image, or nothing happens, you’re asking customers to memorize it.
  • Open the menu. Does it open, and can you reach every page?

02 / The Speed Test (2 minutes)

Slow sites lose visitors before they see anything. Google’s own target is for the main content of a page to appear within 2.5 seconds.2 Your office Wi-Fi will flatter your site, so don’t judge by feel.

Run your address through our free speed test (it uses Google’s own measuring tool) and look at the mobile score. As a rough guide: 90 or above is good, 50 to 89 needs work, and below 50 is costing you customers. If you want the full story on why this matters, see The 3-Second Rule.

03 / The Padlock (30 seconds)

Look at the address bar. Your site should start with https:// and show a padlock or similar icon. Since 2018, Google Chrome has labelled sites without it “Not secure”3, which is not what you want a customer to see right before they type their phone number into your contact form.

Then try typing your address with http:// at the front. It should jump to the https version on its own. If it doesn’t, or you get a warning page, that’s a fix for your web person, and usually a quick one.

04 / The Contact Form (2 minutes)

This is the most important check on the list, because when it fails, nobody tells you. A customer fills in your form, gets a polite “Thank you,” and the message goes nowhere. They assume you weren’t interested and call someone else.

  • Send yourself a test. Fill in your own form with “TEST, please ignore” and submit it.
  • Check that it arrived. Look in your inbox and your spam folder. Note how long it took.
  • Check where it went. Forms are often set up to email an old address, an ex-employee, or the designer who built the site.
  • Check the email links too. If your site lists an email address, tap it on your phone and make sure it opens a new message to the right address.

“A broken contact form doesn’t send you an error message. It just sends your customers to someone else.”

05 / Is the Information Right? (1 minute)

Read your site the way a new customer would, and compare it against your Google Business Profile. Look at:

  • Hours, phone number and address. They should match your Google profile exactly. Mismatched details confuse customers and Google alike.
  • Services and prices. Anything you no longer offer, or prices that went up two years ago, sets up an awkward phone call.
  • Staff and photos. People who left, a storefront you moved out of, a truck you sold.
  • The copyright year in the footer. It’s a small thing, but “© 2021” quietly tells visitors nobody is minding the site.

06 / Can Google Find You? (1 minute)

Two quick searches on Google:

  • Type site:yourbusiness.ca (with your own address, no spaces). This shows pages from your site that Google knows about. Google notes the list isn’t complete,4 but if you get nothing back, or only one page out of ten, something is wrong.
  • Search your business name plus your town. Your website should come up near the top. If a directory listing or an old Facebook page beats it, your site isn’t doing its job.

If you want to go one step further, try searching what a customer would actually type, like “electrician Abbotsford.” Don’t panic if you’re not on page one; that takes ongoing work. But it’s useful to know where you stand.

07 / Look for Broken Things (1 minute)

Click every link in your main menu and footer, on your laptop this time. You’re looking for:

  • “Page not found” errors on pages that used to exist.
  • Images that don’t load and show a blank box or broken icon.
  • Dead social links. A Facebook icon pointing to a page you deleted, or a Twitter link to an account you haven’t touched since 2019.
  • Leftovers. “Lorem ipsum” placeholder text, a “Coming soon” page that never came, or a holiday notice from last December.

08 / Who Holds the Keys? (1 minute)

This one isn’t on the screen, but it’s the check that causes the most grief when it fails. Answer these honestly:

  • Who is your domain name registered to? It should be you or your business, not your designer, a former partner or a relative who “set it up.”
  • Do you have the login for your domain registrar and your hosting? Not “my web guy has it.” You.
  • When does the domain renew, and is the card on file still valid? Domains that lapse can go offline, and occasionally get bought by someone else.

If you can’t answer all three, fix that before anything else on this list. We explain why in What Happens When You Stop Paying Your Web Designer.

Several of these are five-minute fixes you can do yourself, like updating hours or replacing a dead link. Others, like speed or a site that doesn’t work on phones, usually mean the site needs real work.

10 / The Bottom Line

A website rarely fails all at once. It drifts: a form stops sending, the hours change, a page breaks, and nobody notices because nobody is looking. Ten minutes of checking twice a year catches most of it.

Put a reminder in your calendar, run through this list, and fix the urgent items first. If you’d rather have someone do it for you, or you’d like a second opinion on what you found, we’re happy to take a look.
