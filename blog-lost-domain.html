I Lost a Domain to a Pirate Movie Site.
Don’t Let It Happen to You.

By P. Beal — 28 years in banking & financial planning, 19 years as a small business owner, Fraser Valley, BC

Quick Read — Key Takeaways
  • You don’t own your domain outright. You rent it, one renewal at a time.
  • When a renewal is missed, automated buyers can grab the name within days of it being released, then resell it or use it for spam and scams.
  • Almost every lost domain has the same cause: the renewal warnings went to an email address nobody checks.
  • Keep your contact email current, check the card on file, turn on auto-renew and renew for several years at a time.
  • Make sure the domain is registered in your name and that you have the login yourself.

I’ll start with an admission that’s a little embarrassing for someone who builds websites for a living: I lost one of my own domains. I’d been holding it for a future project. Life got busy, the renewal date came and went, and I didn’t notice. When I finally went to use it, I typed the address into my browser and landed on an overseas website streaming pirated movies.

Someone had grabbed it the moment it became available. When I looked into getting it back, the answer was simple: I could buy it back, for what amounted to a ransom. I didn’t pay it. That domain is gone.

I got off lightly, because it was a spare. Now picture the same thing happening to the domain your customers actually know. Your website, the link on your Google listing, the address on your truck and business cards, and every email your clients send you, all pointing at someone else’s server.

“Your domain isn’t a technical detail. It’s your business’s address, your email and your reputation, rented one year at a time.”

01 / You Don’t Own Your Domain. You Rent It.

This is the part most business owners don’t realize. When you “buy” a domain, you’re really registering it for a set period, usually one to ten years. If you don’t renew it, it eventually goes back into the pool for anyone to register.

And “anyone” includes people who do this for a living. There are services that watch for expiring domains and register them automatically the instant they’re released. They look for names with existing traffic, links and search history, because that history is worth money. Then they either sell the name back to its former owner at a steep markup, or put it to work for spam, scams or, in my case, pirated movies.

02 / What Actually Happens When a Domain Expires

You usually get a few chances before it’s truly gone. The timing depends on your registrar and on the ending (.com, .ca and so on), but it generally runs like this:

StageWhat happensYour options
Before expiryFor .com-style domains, registrars must send reminders about a month and about a week before the renewal date1, to whatever email is on fileRenew normally
ExpiryYour website and email may stop working right away. Some registrars offer a grace window (up to about 45 days for .com-style domains), but that’s their choice, not a guarantee1Renew, if offered
RedemptionFor .com-style domains, a 30-day window where it can still be restored1. .ca domains have a similar redemption stage run by CIRA2Restore, usually with a recovery fee
ReleaseThe domain is deleted and released to the public. For .ca, CIRA releases names in a weekly batch, and buyers are waiting2Negotiate with whoever got it

The email problem nobody thinks about. Whoever owns your old domain can set up email on it. Messages your customers, suppliers or bank send to your old address could land in a stranger’s inbox. Losing a domain isn’t just embarrassing; it can be a real security risk.

03 / Good Contact With Your Domain Provider Is the Whole Game

Almost every lost-domain story has the same root cause, and it isn’t carelessness. It’s that the warnings went somewhere nobody was looking. The usual culprits:

  • The contact email is out of date. An old Hotmail account, a former employee’s address, or the email of whoever built your site five years ago. The registrar did its job; the reminders just went into a void.
  • The card on file expired. Auto-renew is switched on, so you think you’re covered. Then your card gets replaced, the renewal payment fails, and the failure notice goes to that same old email address.
  • Someone else owns it on paper. If your web designer, a relative or a past business partner registered the domain in their own account, you’re depending on them to notice. If they move on, retire or simply forget, so does your domain.
  • The real reminder looked like spam. Fake “domain renewal” letters and emails are so common that many owners ignore all of them, including the genuine ones. Know who your actual registrar is so you can tell the difference.

04 / The 10-Minute Domain Checkup

Do this today. Put the kettle on, log into your domain account and work through the list.

  • Know where it lives. Find out which company your domain is registered with, and make sure you can log in to that account yourself.
  • Make sure it’s in your name. The registrant should be you or your business, not your designer, nephew or old business partner.
  • Update the contact email. Use an address you check every day, ideally one that is not on the domain itself. If the domain lapses, that inbox goes down with it.
  • Turn on auto-renew, and check the card. Confirm the payment method is current, and set a reminder for when that card expires.
  • Renew for several years. Most domains cost less per year than lunch out. Locking in five or ten years removes most of the risk.
  • Add your own calendar reminder two months before expiry. Don’t rely only on the registrar’s emails.
  • Turn on the registrar lock and two-step login. This stops anyone from transferring your domain away without your say-so.
  • Grab the obvious variations. If you’re on .com, consider the .ca too, and vice versa, especially if you serve Canadian customers.

Your domain is one of three things that decide whether you really control your website. We cover the other two in What Happens When You Stop Paying Your Web Designer, and the full owner’s checklist is in Check Your Own Website in 10 Minutes.

05 / The Bottom Line

Your website can be rebuilt. Your logo can be redrawn. But once your domain is in someone else’s hands, getting it back is entirely on their terms, and as I found out, those terms can be ugly.

It costs a few dollars a year and ten minutes of attention to make sure that never happens. That’s about the cheapest insurance your business will ever buy.
